In the pilot episode of the critically acclaimed HBO series "Girls", Lena Dunham's character, Hannah, drops the now iconic line - "I think that I may be the voice of my generation. Or at least a voice - of a generation". This line was heavily debated online, with opinions varying from it (and her character) being vain and self-obsessed to the line being an encapsulation of the millennial soul in the age of the internet and social media. I did not fact-check that, I just remember reading a lot of opinions and this stood out to me.

I think I started this blog for simple enough reasons - I wanted to spell my thoughts out loud, harness a creative outlet, talk to people on the internet without being assaulted by ads, subscription fees and forcibly funding evil entities. Well, I guess it's very one-side at the moment, but I'll figure out a comment section soon, hopefully. But why should my voice belong out there? Do I really need to justify the existence of a free GitHub pages blog? I was thinking about why my point of view deserves this kind of space and came to a lukewarm conclusion - If not anything else, my perspective is at least unique. And also, I'm enjoying writing this, and the reality that only a few people will read it is also strangely freeing.

Anyway, you're probably wondering why there's a picture of a bat at the top of this page. I think they're a lot cuter than we give them credit for. The reason is a small excerpt from a paper called "What Is It Like To Be A Bat?" by American philosopher, Thomas Nagel. Maybe I'll actually read the paper some day, but for now this line from the wikipedia article intrigued me the most:

Nagel argues that while a human might be able to imagine what it is like to be a bat by taking "the bat's point of view", it would still be impossible "to know what it is like for a bat to be a bat".

This same thought occurred to me various times in my adult life, but more often than not, when I was talking to my female friends. I learned that as a South Indian man (And I imagine this extends to privileged straight men across the world), I've been quite heavily sheltered from, and even more oblivious to, the lived experiences of women - Even those close to me. This was even taken a step further in the book "Invisible Women" by Caroline Criado-Perez - Which brilliantly details women's experiences living in a world created by and for men like me. And while I can never know what it's like for a woman to be a woman (Or a queer person or a Native American person and so on), learning about their experience was incredibly meaningful to me.

And so I decided (still very self-obsessed-ly) that it's worth putting my perspective on some things out there, even if I can just have a small invisible connection to someone else out there. And yes, there's plenty of perspectives of a young straight Indian man out there and a lot of it could even be problematic. But if it helps, I'm going to write as a straight Indian man living in Los Angeles, who works in the VFX industry in LA, plays guitar, loves movies, and is simultaneously disenfranchised and trying to find a political voice and presence in an increasingly scary authoritarian world.

I want to keep these short, quick, and impulsive without overthinking anything, so we'll part ways here. Currently I don't have a comment section feature or anything set up, but you can use the little email button at the bottom to get in touch if you'd like to discuss anything! Thank you so much for reading this! With maximum love,

RS
