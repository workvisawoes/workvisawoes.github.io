My first inkling of starting a blog occurred to me the day that the known and reviled Pedophile and conman-criminal Donald Trump signed a presidential executive order that would mandate a 100000 dollar fee for new H1B visa applications. First, I wondered if American citizens and permanent residents would even see or know about this news. Second, I wondered if other H1B visa workers like me had the ability to scrounge up anything even close to 100000 dollars. For my part, I could accumulate all the money in my name, and I wouldn't make even half that amount. And I have a pretty well paying job in the very low 6 figures. Third I felt that familiar wave of terror...

It was far from the first time I've felt it, and I can reassure you - every H1B worker who immigrated to the USA after 2001 has now learned this feeling to a terribly deep level of familiarity. The feeling that on a personal, professional, systemic and governmental level - you are not wanted here - and yet you continue to feed the very machine that wants to toss you back into the ocean, assuming you can swim back across continents to "wherever you came from". There are an estimated 399000 active H1B visa workers in the USA as of 2024 but that feeling is always felt in isolation. After that feeling subsided a little, I decided I wanted to make a space on the internet where people could read a little more about the everyday life of a H1B worker without having to wade through a comment section full of hateful racial slurs and half-baked anti-immigrant sentiments. So here's part one!

To start us off on a fair (honestly a tad self-deprecating) note - I want to tell you, reader, that immigrating to the US as a "skilled worker" is a VERY well established pipeline. If you're from India, like I am, it's a near automated process. Here's a quick breakdown:

  • Apply to good colleges
  • Get Accepted
  • Get your F1 student visa
  • Apply to good colleges
  • Get Accepted
  • Get your F1 student visa
  • Graduate with a Master's degree in a STEM field
  • Apply for 1 year of work authorization under OPT and extend it by 2 years under STEM OPT
  • In that time, convince your employer to sponsor your H1B visa
  • Get selected in the H1B lottery and get the visa approved (valid for a maximum of 6 years)
  • In that time, convince your employer to apply for your permanent residence (which takes upto 3 years)
  • If all of the above goes smoothly, you get to stand in line for a green card which could take up to 20 more years

This is a long, painful, tedious, and extremely error-prone and politically unstable process - and as a H1B worker, you learn to know it like the back of your hand. We have seen fully licensed immigration lawyers be defeated and exasperated at how needlessly complicated this process is. Indians make up the largest proportion of H1B workers in the USA with Chinese people being a close second place. This position of relative "comfort" in jumping continents is the tip of a decades-long series of immigration laws, amendments and changes driven not by a sense of "opening borders" or "globalization", but by a very strong demand for skilled labor from foreign lands. Does this not sound like something the American people would want? The first H1 visa for skilled workers was signed into law in 1952 by overriding a presidential veto - requiring at least a 75% majority vote across the aisle in favor. Clearly this was not an unpopular direction. This skilled worker H1 visa was split into two categories by George W Bush in 1990 to make the H1-A visa (exclusively for nurses) and the H1-B visa which remains to day for "workers in specialty occupations". Now what makes our occupations a "specialty"? - well that's a whole other rabbit hole we can't get into now. But funny how temporary agricultural workers, who are nearly equal in number (398000 confirmed in 2025) are not eligible, but are instead brought in on H2A visas which do not offer the same prevailing wage protections, job portability or grace periods while out of status. But I get all of that because I can write code while killing every one of my houseplants.

Pardon that digression. The point I want to get across here is that when a young worker makes the decision to immigrate to the USA, they don't really have the perspective of the history of policy or corporate machinery that enables them to do so.Instead, we have a robust community of immigrants that have made the exact same journey and the safety of being able to follow (exactly) in their footsteps without being able to see what they are seeing. A young college graduate in India simply has options of pursuing employment for a living back home or taking the leap to go overseas to pursue a Master's degree ora PhD. This could be a very familiar path previously tread by people you know or a gigantic leap of faith into a strange and unwelcoming land. But once you're in it, it's a well established pipeline. A pipeline, I have to remind my self, that is only accessible to the most privileged of people - relatively rich members of India's Hindu majority.

You finish your Master's degree with debilitating debt and an always unforgiving job market - but every step of paperwork to get you from a graduate to a 3-year STEM OPT worker to your first H1-B filing through to your spot in the green card queue is documented and certified by a generation of people who have done it before you. The internet has an answer to fill every crack in the system and a reddit post for every misfiled document. There are backup plans and backup plans to those backup plans. It all feels immediately necessary and easy to do - which makes sense when you graduate in a class with many people going through the exact same process. You go about asserting your legal right to stay in the country as if it is just the next task to do. It would never occur to us until very late into the process that we were signing up for decades of paying taxes to a government that is only interested in extracting our labor for the lowest "legal" wage and will NEVER accept our vote.

H1B visa workers come to the USA with a mindset that's a mixture of many ideas - "I'm here to learn and seek opportunity and a better life" -"I'm here to take advantage of a fairly liberal immigration system" - "I'm here to chase my dream of writing code in Silicon Valley" - "I'm here because I hate it back home". It was simply a fork in the road that looked very pretty to walk down and no one realizes that it could cave underneath your feet at the slightest whim of your employer or the diaper-wearing sex offender in the White House that they report to. Any honesty or purity to the motivation is soon trampled over by a suffocating system that threatens deportation at the slightest misstep. Now the chest-thumping red-blooded "real" Americans may have you know think - well that's the idea. Your visa is contingent on your employment and that's that. But if we really put that system to the test, you realize that employment connection is tenuous at best - particularly in a hyper-capitalist country like the USA.

If we were to argue that our right to reside in the USA is in exchange for contributing to the USA's economy - every H1B worker deserves residence in perpetuity because we contribute to the economy in the two most primarily effective avenues: paying taxes and consuming from local, national and international businesses. This is of course in addition to doing our jobs. A 2024 study by a private immigration consultancy called "Lighthouse" estimated that every year, H1B workers contribute 84 billion dollars to the economy, pay 26 billion dollars in federal taxes and 11 billion dollars in state and local taxes. We pay our taxes but get none of the benefits of doing so. We do not qualify for any medical or unemployment benefits. Our visa mandates that we can only work in jobs strictly relevant to our college degrees which restricts to an ever shrinking job pool in an ever expanding worker population. We obviously cannot vote in any local or federal elections whatsoever rendering us puppets of a system that we don't get a voice in. But any H1B worker would tell you, we're here for that. We signed up for it. The average Indian H1B worker LOVES national parks and has definitely been to Yosemite multiple times. We are very happy to pay for Yosemite staying open and running smoothly. Guess who actually cut funding to the National Park Service though?

Now before I dig myself into a hole, I want to quickly remind myself and you that we live in a world where all countries and borders not made equal - far from it. Borders are only tools of oppression and exclusion and no human being can be declared "illegal". So to address the ever looming question - "Why can't we just go back home?". Sure we can. We can go any time. We can go back to our homeland which has been exploited, ravaged, pillaged and raped by the USA and the UK and it's allies - subjected to centuries of violent colonial rule and then divided by arbitrarily sketched borders - abused for its large and ready workforce that makes the clothes you wear, mines the Aluminium in your laptops, and grows the spices that go to waste in your kitchens. I can go back to my hometown where, even in the richest cities, pursuing the same dream is so economically infeasible, it's actively discouraged and the post-colonial hangover from 1947 still requires centuries of slow and painful recovery in the face of suffocating tariffs, sanctions and widespread racism from the west.

We can - but we won't - because, fuck you.

I'll wind up on that rather inflammatory note with just another reminder. The reason I want to put forward a H1B POV is to show you how insignificant it is in the face of the corporate powers that exploit it. These powers don't WANT immigrants, they want cheap labor that they can control and systemically oppress - and the American worker has freedoms that protect them from this oppression while the immigrant does not. "Legal" and "Illegal" workers, documented and undocumented workers still remain under the thumb of corporate oligarchs and the corrupt governments that support them.
