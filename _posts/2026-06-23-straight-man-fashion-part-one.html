I re-watched The Devil Wears Prada recently in anticipation of the sequel coming out in theaters. I vaguely recall my initial reaction to the character of Miranda Priestly was unequivocal condemnation - I didn't care what level of genius, vision or insight she may have or claimed to have. Nothing could possibly justify her horrible disregard for the people around her - least of all, some tirade about how Anne Hathaway's Cerulean sweater was tacky and predictable. That was of course my FIRST impression. Upon rewatch, this flashy, self-aggrandizing monologue was suddenly far more scary than it was pompous or condescending.

In the scene - Miranda Priestly, the high-powered creative head of Runway fashion magazine, harshly, but methodically schools her new intern Andy on the power and reach of the fashion world. She asserts how Andy's choice of a Cerulean blue sweater was not an independent choice and expression of taste as much as it was ultimately dictated by powerful trends put in place by people like herself and the powers that be at Runway magazine. Watching the movie, this scene completely undercuts Andy's (and my) disdain for the fashion world and acts a reminder of Miranda's knowledge, power, and influence.

A little more prying and critical thinking will reveal how this scene is unfairly biased against Andy. She joins Runway magazine as her only option for a career in journalism and considers it a temporary stepping stone and is spited for it. Her outfit looks badly put together in contrast with the fashion veterans that are her colleagues - all of whom stare at her with a touch of pity but mostly embarrassment. But why am I bringing up this scene in a post about straight men fashion?

My theory is that lot of straight men identify with Andy. We feel a distance from the world of fashion industry and advertisement and that distance comes with varied feelings. I have felt Andy's sense of pride in not "succumbing" to fashion trends and not letting them inform my consumption. I have also felt her sense of shame when my casual approach to an outfit has made me stand out badly in an old photograph or not match up to my sharply dressed friends. I'm willing to bet that a lot of straight men feel a similar sense of self-satisfaction in the fact that they never have to look very hard to find clothes that they like. We have a very comfortable and familiar bedrock in "T-shirt and Jeans" that we feel enormous safety in and that hasn't changed for me since middle school.

Now, upon rewatch of this movie with a fully developed adult brain, those same feelings of empathy for Andy were replaced with a sense of horror and disbelief. I have been through a long and fulfilling journey of developing my own style - but was that all foretold by the Miranda Priestlys of the world? Is my ideal, perfectly fitting pair of slim jeans at Hollister just the norm for any five-and-a-half foot dude? I recently went out of my way to buy a pair of wide-legged pants (SO not my style) just to try and broaden my horizons. I hear wide-legged pants are "in" these days- and I felt like I took a step in a new direction. But those pants were SO EASY to find - they were on display and on sale at the Men's section in Uniqlo. Was this the plan the whole time? Did I just check a box somewhere, confirming someone's idea of what a young man gravitates towards in a store? The very thought seems so debilitating and leaves me with such a vacuum of agency.

This will be part one of a series where I badly self-therapize through writing by exploring my fashion from toddler to thirty-year-old and try to uncover the balance of power and perception that make me reach for my jeans and T-shirt every day. I'd love to hear all your thoughts on how you formed your fashion identity in the face of this giant consumerism machine and what powers and people influenced it.
